01 The Hypothesis

What is AI doing to jobs in India,
right now?

TCS cut 23,460 jobs this year. Infosys's under-30 workforce just hit a 15-year low. This page looks at what's happening in India's job market right now, and what we still don't know.

02 The Original Method

What was the Billion Prices Project?

Inspiration

We're borrowing an idea from economics. In 2008, two researchers stopped waiting for official inflation numbers and started measuring prices themselves.

Background

Alberto Cavallo and Roberto Rigobon began collecting hundreds of millions of prices from online retailers around the world. They used this to build daily inflation estimates without surveys, government offices, or delays. Read the paper here. In 2011, their method showed that Argentina's official inflation rate was about a third of what their data actually found, which turned an academic project into a real check on government statistics.

We think the same idea applies to jobs. Official employment numbers come out far less often than prices ever did, and they get disputed too. If a government can lag behind or misstate something as basic as prices, it's likely to lag even further behind on something as new as AI.

→

For example, the two measures matched until about 2010, then separated, with the scraped index staying much higher than the official rate. This shows the general pattern, not the exact published numbers.

04 The Methodology

Four steps between a hypothesis and a working index.

Usually, this step is skipped with a simple slide saying "we'll build an index." Here is what we would actually need to do.

1

Fix the occupation crosswalk.

Exposure scores like AIOE were built on US task data. Pizzinelli et al. mapped them onto India using occupation codes, but that assumes a software developer's job looks the same in Bangalore as it does in Seattle, a stretch, since Indian IT work leans much more toward delivery and execution than the design-heavy roles the US data was built on.

Our fixUse their crosswalk as a starting point, but check a sample of matched jobs against real Indian job listings. Adjust wherever the tasks do not match, starting with IT services roles.
2

Build the job-ad signal.

Collect job postings every month from Naukri, LinkedIn India, and Indeed India. Match each posting to an occupation code using the title and description. Mark those that mention AI skills, starting with clear terms like "GenAI" or "prompt engineering," but also include postings that describe similar work without those exact words. Track how this share changes each month for each occupation, along with any listed salary ranges.

3

Make sure it's actually AI.

We would compare jobs that are more exposed to AI with those that are less exposed, before and after GenAI adoption increased in India, around late 2022 to 2023. This comparison only works if both groups were moving together before that point. If they were not, we cannot confidently say AI caused any changes after. We would also separate the data by state and compare IT hubs to other areas, to make sure we are not confusing the 2023-24 global IT spending slowdown with an AI effect.

4

Check if it's actually working.

Compare our monthly numbers to the official ones (PLFS every quarter and CMIE every month) and see if our data changes first. The main idea is that a faster index from job ads and hiring data should reveal changes before slow official surveys do, just like scraped prices once outpaced government inflation numbers. The IT hiring slowdown in 2025-26 is a recent example we could use to test this.

Technical appendix: crosswalk tables, regression spec, classifier design+

The following details are technical specifications for building the index. Including them in the main text would distract from the main story.

  • Crosswalk table. Full NCO, ISCO and O*NET mapping for the top 30 to 40 occupations by employment share, with mismatches flagged from the audit step.
  • Regression specification. Functional form, fixed effects (occupation, month, state), clustering approach, and the exact parallel-trends test and plot.
  • Classifier design. Embedding model choice, seed-term list, validation approach (precision and recall on a hand-labeled sample), and how it handles Hindi and regional-language postings.
  • Sensitivity checks. Rerunning the whole pipeline with Eloundou et al.'s GPT-exposure scores instead of AIOE, to see if the conclusions hold under a different exposure measure.
  • Scraping architecture. Platform notes and ToS/legal considerations for Naukri, LinkedIn and Indeed.
  • Statistical power. How many postings and months are needed before the difference-in-differences estimates are reliable.

India is the only country in the study where women have lower AI exposure than men, since many women work in agriculture. In all other countries studied, women have higher AI exposure than men.

The effect comes from hiring fewer new employees, not from layoffs, and is most noticeable in jobs where AI replaces tasks instead of helping with them.

Source: Brynjolfsson, Chandar, Chen, "Canaries in the Coal Mine?" (Stanford, rev. Aug 2026)

Evidence: automate vs. augment

"Automate" means AI does the task instead of a person, while "augment" means a person still does the task with AI's help. The paper's findings only appear in automate-type jobs, which is why there are fewer new hires rather than layoffs. Companies need fewer new employees for tasks that software can now handle completely. The two numbers above (-19% and -20%) are the specific results reported here, but the paper's data covers more groups and jobs than shown.

09 What We Can't See

Every proxy here still stops at the edge of the formal economy.

Population-wide administrative payroll data?

✓
United States (ADP)
Monthly, individual-level, 730 occupations
✓
Denmark (Statistics Denmark)
Linked to worker surveys
–
India (EPFO)
Monthly aggregate by industry only, not individual-level
Evidence: what EPFO actually publishes

EPFO's public data shows monthly counts of new subscribers by industry group, but only at a broad level. There are no records for individual workers or specific jobs, so we cannot identify particular roles like ADP or Statistics Denmark can. This is a structural issue, not just a matter of how often EPFO publishes data.

Using 25,000 workers linked to full administrative wage and hours records, we find precise null effects on earnings and hours two years post-ChatGPT (ruling out effects larger than 2%) despite widespread adoption. What moved instead was task composition and occupational mobility.
Humlum & Vestergaard, "Still Waters, Rapid Currents" (NBER / Rockwool, 2025–26)
Lesson for this pageBe careful not to claim a clear wage effect. Even in countries with much more data than India, such an effect has not been proven.
Evidence: the study design

25,000 Danish workers, linked to Denmark's full administrative wage and hours records (not a survey), tracked for two years after ChatGPT's release. The null result rules out effects larger than plus or minus 2% on earnings and hours specifically; it doesn't rule out smaller effects, and it doesn't cover what happened to task composition or which occupations people moved into, which is exactly what the authors found moved instead.

11 The Verdict

The method works. The data infrastructure doesn't, yet.

  • The method works in theory: Copestake and others have already shown that tracking job ads can detect real AI labor effects in India.
  • The signal is active and visible now: based on Anthropic's India data and numbers from TCS, Infosys, and Xpheno, mainly in IT and urban areas.
  • But India does not have payroll-level data infrastructure like the US and Denmark: any index must rely on indirect and proxy data, and claims about wage effects should be made carefully.
